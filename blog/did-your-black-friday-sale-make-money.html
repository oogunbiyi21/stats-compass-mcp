Did your Black Friday sale actually make money?

By Tunji Ogunbiyi

Black Friday is over for another year. The emails have gone out, the discount codes have been used, and hopefully sales went up. Now many store owners are asking the same thing: was my Black Friday sale actually profitable, or have I just given away margin on sales I'd have had anyway? Sales almost always go up over Black Friday, but that doesn't tell you whether the sale made money. To find out, read this step-by-step guide and use this free promotion profitability calculator.

Why "sales went up" isn't the answer

Late November is busy for almost every store. People start buying gifts, shoppers are already in the habit of browsing, and your emails get more opens. Some of that would have happened with no sale at all.

So the useful question isn't "did sales go up?" it's "how much did sales go up because of the sale, and was that worth the discount?"

Why comparing with the weeks before doesn't work either

The most common check is to compare Black Friday week with a normal week in October. The problem is that October is quieter than late November anyway, so almost any sale looks like a success against it.

Comparing with last year's Black Friday is better, but only if you allow for how much your store has grown (or shrunk) since then. A store that's 30% bigger than last year should beat last year's Black Friday without trying.

Start with what you'd have sold anyway

The fair comparison is an estimate of what you'd have sold that week without the sale. It takes into account three things:

  • The time of year: how much busier late November usually is for your store.
  • Your store's growth: whether you're bigger or smaller than this time last year.
  • Normal ups and downs: weekly sales vary on their own, so the estimate is a range, not one exact number.

The extra sales from your promotion (what marketers call incremental sales, or promotional lift) are what you actually sold minus that expected amount. If actual sales sit inside the normal range, the honest answer is that the sale may not have made a difference.

Here's an example from our test store, which runs on simulated orders. Its SUMMER40 sale (14 May – 3 June 2026) averaged 34% off. Shopify's own report shows sales up 31% on the previous three weeks. This looks like a win. But a 34% discount needs far more than that to pay for itself. Compared with what the store would normally sell at that time of year, the sale needed about 2.1 times normal sales to cover its discount (at the store's margin of about 50%), and it got about 1.3 times. It lost money: it earned about £1,362 less margin than a normal few weeks would have.

A smaller sale in the same store went the other way. AUTUMN15 (15% off, 19–25 October 2026) needed about 1.2 times normal sales to break even and got about 1.6 times, so it most likely paid for itself.

What "made money" really means

Extra sales aren't profit. To know whether the sale made money, three things need to come off:

  • The discount on sales you'd have had anyway. If you'd normally have sold £10,000 that week and everything was 20% off, you gave away £2,000 on orders that would have come in at full price. This is the cost most people miss.
  • Extra ad spend for the sale. If you spent more on ads than usual to promote it, that's part of what the sale cost.
  • Returns. Sale orders are often returned more. Shopify takes returns off on the day they're processed, so later returns show up in the weeks after the sale, which is another reason to check again in mid-December.

The break-even test: how much discount can you afford?

A discount cuts what you earn on every sale, so you need to sell more just to stand still. A simple rule:

Sales needed to break even (in sales value) = margin × (1 − discount) ÷ (margin − discount)

For example, with a 40% margin and 20% off, each item earns half what it normally does (20 instead of 40), so you need to sell twice as many items. But each item also sells for 20% less, so in sales value that's about 1.6 times your normal sales. That's the figure the calculator shows, and it's before any ad spend. Some guides and calculators mix up items and sales value, so check which one you're looking at.

Use the margin on the items that were actually on sale, after product costs, the shipping you pay and payment fees. Your store-wide average can be quite different, especially if the sale pushed people towards low-margin products.

Don't forget the dip afterwards

Some customers didn't buy more because of your sale; they just bought earlier than they would have. When that happens, sales in the weeks after the sale fall below normal, and that dip eats into the gain.

You can only see the dip once two or three weeks have passed. So check now for the lift, and again in mid-December for the dip.

How to check it by hand

  1. Export your daily sales for the last 13 months or so from Shopify (Analytics, then the "Total sales over time" report, by day).
  2. Work out how much your sales grew from last year to this year, using the few months before the sale (for example August to October).
  3. Apply that growth to last year's sales for the same weeks. That's your rough "what I'd have sold anyway" figure.
  4. Subtract it from what you actually sold during the sale. That's the extra sales.
  5. Compare the extra sales with the break-even figure from the table, and take off any extra ad spend.
  6. Two or three weeks later, repeat steps 3 and 4 for the weeks after the sale to see if there was a dip.

This gives you a rough answer. It won't tell you how sure you can be, and it's easy to be fooled by one unusual week.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my Black Friday sale was profitable?

Compare your sales with what you'd have sold that week without the sale, not with the weeks before. Then check that the extra sales covered the discount you gave on all orders, plus any extra ad spend. Check again two or three weeks later for a dip.

How much discount can I afford?

It depends on how much extra the sale brings in. To break even, sales need to reach margin × (1 − discount) ÷ (margin − discount) times normal. With a 40% margin, 20% off needs about 1.6 times normal sales, and 30% off about 2.8 times. If your sales won't grow that much, the discount is too deep.

Why did my sales drop after Black Friday?

Often because some customers bought earlier than they would have, to get the discount. A short dip after a big sale is normal. A long or deep one can mean the sale pulled forward more sales than it created.

Does this work if I lowered prices instead of using a discount code?

Yes. The method uses your total daily sales, so it doesn't matter how the discount was applied. Just enter how much you lowered prices by.

How much sales history do I need?

About a year before the sale, so the comparison can allow for the time of year. With less, the honest answer is that there isn't enough data yet.
